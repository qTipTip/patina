## What is ray tracing?

Ray tracing is basically following a ray of light as it bounces and interacts
with objects.

Some key components: 

- Cameras, where the scene is being viewed from.
- Ray-object intersections, basically linear algebra.
- Light sources, there are many types of lights and sources!
- Visibility, is an object visible at all from the camera?
- Light scattering at surfaces. That is, materials encode some properties of the objects in the scene.
- Indirect light transport, allowing light rays to bounce more than once.
- Ray propagation, what are the properties of the ambient space we're
scattering light through? Is it a foggy room? Or is it a black hole.

## How is `pbrt` structured?

Chapter 1.3 gives a brief overview of the various components that's at play. I
won't go into detail here. Roughly, you can divide execution into three phases:

1. Parse the scene description
2. Initialize the scene
3. Execute the main rendering loop

The chapter also briefly mentions the role of samplers, and how you need
samplers to _approximate_ the
[integral](https://pbr-book.org/4ed/Introduction/Photorealistic_Rendering_and_the_Ray-Tracing_Algorithm#eq:rendering-equation=)
you need to compute for the light transport.

So, the integrator components are stochastic in nature. Rule: _more samples ->
better quality_. Note that we can provide previews of the rendered image by
rendering in waves of a few samples per pixel.

The integrator classes are in `pbrt` designed as subclasses:

> Integrator -> Image Tile Integrator -> Ray Integrator -> Random Walk Integrator

We need to figure out how to structure this in Rust.
